Every project in our portfolio carries a small colored ring with a number inside. That's its PageSpeed score: Google's 0 to 100 grade for how quickly a page loads and how steady it stays while it loads. Here's what the score measures, how to read the ring, and the fixes that move a website into the green.

We'll use our own site as the example. Not long ago, our homepage scored 66 on mobile. Today it scores in the high 90s, and every fix that got it there is below.

What PageSpeed Insights measures

PageSpeed Insights is Google's free speed test. Enter any web address and it loads the page twice: once as a mid-range phone on a slow mobile connection, and once as a desktop computer on a fast connection. Each run gets a performance score from 0 to 100:

  • Good90–100
  • Needs improvement50–89
  • Poor0–49

The testing engine behind it is called Lighthouse. It's also built into Chrome's developer tools, so developers can run the same test while they build.

Why your score matters

  • Visitors don't wait. On a phone, a page that sits blank for a few seconds often loses the visitor to the next search result.
  • Google takes it into account. Google uses real-world speed data, called Core Web Vitals, in its search rankings. Relevant content still matters most, so think of speed as an edge, not a shortcut.
  • It's a first impression. A site that appears instantly and holds still feels as professional as the business behind it.

Mobile vs. desktop scores

The mobile test slows everything down on purpose to match a typical phone on a weak signal, so almost every site scores lower on mobile than on desktop. That's normal, and it's why you should compare mobile to mobile and desktop to desktop.

The rings on our project pages show the desktop result, and each project page labels it that way.

The five measurements behind the score

The score isn't a single stopwatch reading. It's a weighted blend of five measurements, each graded on its own. Here they are in the order they appear around our rings, starting at the top and going clockwise:

FCP First Contentful Paint 10% of the score

How long until the first text or image appears. It's the moment a visitor knows the page is working.

Green at about 0.9 s or less on desktop, 1.8 s or less on mobile

LCP Largest Contentful Paint 25% of the score

How long until the largest thing on the screen, usually the main photo or headline, has finished loading. This is when the page feels loaded.

Green at 1.2 s or less on desktop, 2.5 s or less on mobile

TBT Total Blocking Time 30% of the score

How long the page is too busy running code to respond to a tap or click while it loads. It's the biggest slice of the score, and it's where heavy scripts, plugins, and widgets cost the most.

Green at 150 ms or less on desktop, 200 ms or less on mobile

CLS Cumulative Layout Shift 25% of the score

How much the page jumps around while it loads, like a button that moves just as you go to tap it. It's measured as a score, not a time, and lower is better.

Green at 0.1 or less on desktop and mobile

SI Speed Index 10% of the score

How quickly the visible part of the page fills in overall, not just its first or largest piece.

Green at about 1.3 s or less on desktop, 3.4 s or less on mobile

How to read the rings on our project pages

Now the ring makes sense. The number in the middle is the score. The ring around it is cut into five arcs, one per measurement. Each arc is as long as that measurement's share of the score, so Total Blocking Time gets the longest one, and each arc fills up as far as the site scored on it, in Google's colors. A complete green ring means every measurement passed.

How to raise your score

These are the fixes that move the numbers the most, roughly in the order we'd tackle them.

1. Shrink your images

Images are usually the heaviest part of a page and the most common reason for a slow Largest Contentful Paint.

  • Save photos as WebP (or AVIF) instead of PNG or full-size JPG. They're often a fraction of the size with no visible difference.
  • Resize each image to the largest size it's actually shown, and give phones a smaller copy (the srcset attribute does this).
  • Let images further down the page wait until the visitor scrolls near them (loading="lazy"), but never the main image at the top. Tell the browser to fetch that one first (fetchpriority="high").
  • Give every image a width and height so the browser can save its space before it arrives.

From our own site: when our homepage scored 66 on mobile, its main content took 10.8 seconds to appear on the test phone. The page weighed about 6.9 MB, mostly images, including a 1 MB logo. Today each project screenshot is a WebP file under 40 KB, and the logo is text styled with CSS, so there's no logo image to download at all.

2. Go easy on fonts

Custom fonts look great, but each one is a file the browser has to fetch before it can draw your text the way you designed it.

  • Stick to one or two font families, and only the weights you actually use.
  • Host font files on your own site instead of loading them from a font service, which costs an extra connection to someone else's server.
  • Use the compact WOFF2 format, preload your main font file, and set font-display: swap so text appears right away in a backup font.

From our own site: loading our font from Google Fonts held up the first paint by about 1.5 seconds on phones. Now it's a single 27 KB file served from our own site.

3. Cut back on JavaScript

Every script has to download and then run, and while it runs, the page can't respond. That's where Total Blocking Time comes from, and it's where page builders and plugin-heavy sites lose the most points.

  • Remove scripts and plugins you don't need, like an entire library loaded for one slideshow.
  • Load the rest with defer so they don't hold up the page.
  • Avoid code that measures the page while it's still loading. Each measurement forces the browser to stop and recalculate the layout.

From our own site: one small check that measured our project carousel while the page loaded cost about 170 ms of blocking time on phones. Fixing it, along with the font change above, helped take our homepage from 90 to 98 on mobile.

4. Be choosy about add-ons

Chat bubbles, review widgets, social feeds, embedded maps and videos, and tracking pixels all load code from someone else's server, and you can't make their code faster.

  • Keep the ones that earn their place.
  • Load the rest after the page is ready, or show a lightweight preview, like a video thumbnail, that loads the real thing only when it's clicked.

5. Keep things from jumping around

Layout shift is often the quickest of the five to fix:

  • Give every image, video, and embed a set size, or at least a shape (an aspect ratio), so its space is held before it loads.
  • Don't push content down after the page appears, like a banner that slides in above everything.
  • Animate by moving and fading things (CSS transform and opacity) instead of changing their size or position on the page.

From our own site: our menu bar floats at the top of the page, then stretches edge to edge once you scroll. Resizing it the obvious way would have counted as layout shift every time, so it changes shape with clipping and movement instead, and the page's layout shift stays at zero.

6. Start with fast hosting

Nothing appears until the server answers, so a slow host drags down every other number.

  • Choose hosting with quick response times, and turn on compression (Brotli or gzip) and caching.
  • If your customers are spread out, use a CDN so files come from a server near them.
  • Keep pages lean. The less there is to send, the sooner it arrives.

How to test your own site

  1. Go to pagespeed.web.dev, enter your address, and run the test.
  2. Check both the Mobile and Desktop tabs. Mobile is the tougher test, and it's how many of your customers will see your site.
  3. Run it three times and go with the middle score. Results naturally move a few points between runs.
  4. If your site gets enough visitors using Chrome, the top of the report also shows how real people experienced it over the past 28 days. That real-world data is what Google's rankings use. Smaller sites often don't have enough traffic for it, which makes the lab test your best guide.
  5. Scroll past the score for the specific issues slowing your page down. Fix the biggest ones first, then test again.

Don't chase 100 at all costs

A score in the 90s is excellent. Past that point, a feature that brings in customers, like a booking calendar or a map, is worth more than the last point or two. Speed supports clear content and an easy way to reach you; it doesn't replace them. The goal is a site that feels instant to your customers.
